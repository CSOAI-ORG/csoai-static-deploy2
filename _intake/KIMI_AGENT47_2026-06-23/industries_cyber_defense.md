## 1.5 Cross-Border Scenarios

### Scenario 1: EU-US Data Transfer for AI Threat Detection
A US-based XDR provider (CrowdStrike/Palo Alto) processes European enterprise security telemetry in US cloud data centers. Must comply with EU NIS2 (cybersecurity measures, 24h incident reporting), EU GDPR (data transfer safeguards), and US CLOUD Act (government data access). The EU-US Data Privacy Framework provides adequacy, but NIS2 cybersecurity risk management measures and incident reporting obligations still apply.

### Scenario 2: Chinese MLPS 2.0 + Foreign AI Cybersecurity Tool Deployment
A multinational bank deploys AI-powered threat detection tools across its China operations. The tools must comply with China's MLPS 2.0 (Level 3 assessment for financial systems, annual PSB reviews), the DSL (important data classification), and PIPL (cross-border security assessment for data export). Foreign encryption products require approval under the Cryptography Law.

### Scenario 3: APAC Multi-Jurisdiction SOAR Deployment
A Singapore-headquartered MSSP operates AI-driven SOAR platforms across AU (SOCI Act CIRMP), JP (APPI), KR (AI Framework Act + PIPA), and IN (CERT-In directions + DPDPA). Each jurisdiction has different incident reporting timelines: AU (SOCI: immediate for critical), IN (CERT-In: 6 hours), KR (AI Act: varies), creating a complex compliance matrix for automated incident response workflows.

## 2.5 Cross-Border Scenarios

### Scenario 1: US-EU Defense AI Technology Transfer (ITAR + Dual-Use)
A US company develops AI-powered drone swarm technology for a NATO ally. Must navigate ITAR (22 CFR) for defense article classification, apply for DDTC export license, and comply with EU Dual-Use Regulation (2029/821) upon import into the EU. The 2024 Space 1/2/3 rules may reclassify some components from ITAR to EAR if civilian-use predominant. European recipient must comply with EU AI Act if system has dual civilian applications.

### Scenario 2: Autonomous Weapons System Export to Middle East
A UK-based defense contractor sells AI-enhanced targeting systems to a UAE customer. Must comply with UK Strategic Export Licensing (ECJU), EU Dual-Use Regulation post-Brexit arrangements, MTCR Category I restrictions for certain technologies, and UAE Federal Cybercrime Law (DL 34/2021) for system operation. Wassenaar Arrangement controls on advanced AI targeting software may apply.

### Scenario 3: AI Defense Software Development Across Five Eyes
A Five Eyes consortium (US-UK-CA-AU-NZ) jointly develops battlefield AI decision-support software. Must satisfy US ITAR/EAR for shared codebase, UK MoD security requirements, Australia's SOCI Act for hosting infrastructure, Canadian export controls under Export and Import Permits Act, and NZ's Customs and Excise Act. Each jurisdiction's defense classification system must be harmonized for cross-border development.

## 3.5 Cross-Border Scenarios

### Scenario 1: EU-China Smart City Surveillance Partnership
A Chinese smart city AI vendor (Hikvision/Dahua) wants to deploy facial recognition in an EU member state's smart city project. EU AI Act **prohibits** real-time biometric identification in public spaces (Art. 5(1)(h)) and emotion recognition (Art. 5(1)(f)). The Chinese company's product may also violate GDPR Art. 9 (special category data). Chinese MLPS 2.0 and data security law requirements for product security must also be met for the China-manufactured devices. This creates a **complete incompatibility** between Chinese permissive approach and EU prohibitive approach.

### Scenario 2: Five Eyes Predictive Policing Data Sharing
Five Eyes intelligence agencies share AI-powered predictive analytics for counter-terrorism. Predictive criminal risk assessment based solely on profiling is **prohibited by EU AI Act Art. 5(1)(d)**. UK Investigatory Powers Act permits bulk data collection. US agencies operate under EO 14110 guidelines. This creates tension: EU citizens' data processed in predictive systems may violate EU AI Act if used by EU partners, even if collected by non-EU agencies.

### Scenario 3: Cross-Border Facial Recognition Database (Airport/Border)
An international airport operator deploys facial recognition for passenger processing across EU, UAE, and Asian hubs. EU AI Act requires explicit consent or specific legal basis; prohibits biometric categorization (Art. 5(1)(g)). UAE Safe City initiatives mandate surveillance. Singapore requires PDPA consent. The system must simultaneously satisfy: (1) EU prohibition on biometric categorization, (2) UAE surveillance requirements, (3) Singapore consent requirements, and (4) airline ICAO standards - an extremely complex compliance matrix.

## 5.5 Cross-Border Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Multi-National Power Grid AI Protection (EU + US + Asia)
A global energy company operates power grids across EU (Germany, France), US (Texas), and Asia (India, Japan). Must comply simultaneously with: EU NIS2 (cybersecurity risk mgmt, 24h reporting), US NERC CIP (mandatory standards, audits), Japan CII Protection Act (sector-specific requirements), India CERT-In (6-hour reporting). AI-powered grid management systems trigger EU AI Act high-risk classification (critical infrastructure). Cross-border data flows for AI training data require MLPS 2.0 compliance in China (if China operations exist) and GDPR compliance for EU data.

### Scenario 2: Nuclear Facility AI Safety System (ITAR + National Security)
A nuclear power operator deploys AI-powered anomaly detection for reactor safety systems. US-origin AI components may trigger ITAR/EAR controls. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (10 CFR 73.54) cybersecurity requirements apply. Cross-border technical support from vendor may require export licenses. EU operations trigger EURATOM safety requirements plus NIS2. Post-incident data sharing with international nuclear regulators (IAEA) may conflict with national security classification requirements.

### Scenario 3: Smart City Water System AI (Australia SOCI + Multi-Jurisdiction)
A smart water management system with AI-powered leak detection and quality monitoring is deployed across Australian, UK, and Canadian cities. Australian SOCI Act requires CIRMP and incident reporting. UK Water Services Regulation Authority (Ofwat) has cybersecurity expectations. Canada's Bill C-8 (when enacted) will mandate critical cyber systems protection. IoT sensors sourced from China trigger MLPS 2.0 and potential supply chain security concerns under all three jurisdictions' frameworks. Cross-border data sharing for AI model training requires compliance with three different data protection regimes.

## 6.5 Cross-Border Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Foundation Model Provider Serving EU + US + Asia
A US-based foundation model provider (e.g., GPT-4 class) serves customers in EU, US, Japan, and South Korea. Must comply with: EU AI Act (GPAI model obligations from 2 Aug 2025: technical documentation, systemic risk evaluation, red-team testing); US EO 14110 (federal agency risk management); Japan AI Guidelines (human-centric principles); South Korea AI Framework Act (effective Jan 2026: transparency, human oversight). Model training data may include personal data triggering GDPR, APPI, PIPA. Each jurisdiction has different red-teaming and evaluation requirements.

### Scenario 2: Healthcare AI Diagnostic Tool (EU AI Act High-Risk + Multi-Jurisdiction)
An AI-powered medical diagnostic tool is deployed across EU, UK, US, and Australia. EU AI Act classifies as high-risk (healthcare, Annex III): requires conformity assessment, CE marking, risk management system, post-market monitoring. US FDA may require 510(k) clearance or De Novo classification. UK MHRA has separate AI medical device guidance. Australia TGA has software as medical device requirements. Post-market AI incident reporting timelines differ: EU (immediate for serious incidents), US (MDR regulations), UK (vigilance requirements), AU (TGA reporting).

### Scenario 3: AI-Powered Credit Scoring (Multi-Jurisdiction Automated Decision-Making)
A fintech company deploys AI credit scoring across EU, Brazil, India, and Singapore. EU AI Act classifies as high-risk (credit scoring, Annex III) plus GDPR Art. 22 automated decision-making safeguards. Brazil AI Bill (when enacted) will require transparency in credit decisions. India DPDPA has data principal rights for automated processing. Singapore MAS FEAT Principles require explainability and fairness. Each jurisdiction has different requirements for human-in-the-loop, explainability, and recourse - creating a complex governance matrix for a single AI system.
