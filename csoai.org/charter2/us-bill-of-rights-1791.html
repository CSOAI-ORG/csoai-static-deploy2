Preamble

The Bill of Rights (15 December 1791) comprises the first ten amendments to the US Constitution. Ratified on 15 December 1791, two years after the Constitution itself, it represents the Anti-Federalist compromise that secured ratification. The amendments descend directly from the British Bill of Rights 1689, the Magna Carta 1215, and the Levellers' Agreement of the People 1647.

The Ten Amendments (verbatim selection)

  1. First Amendment: "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances."
  2. Second Amendment: "A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed."
  3. Third Amendment: "No Soldier shall, in time of peace be quartered in any house, without the consent of the Owner, nor in time of war, but in a manner to be prescribed by law."
  4. Fourth Amendment: "The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated..."
  5. Fifth Amendment: Due process; double jeopardy; self-incrimination; eminent domain with just compensation.
  6. Sixth Amendment: Speedy trial; public trial; impartial jury; confrontation of witnesses; counsel.
  7. Seventh Amendment: Jury trial in civil cases.
  8. Eighth Amendment: "Excessive bail shall not be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel and unusual punishments inflicted." (Direct lift from English Bill of Rights 1689.)
  9. Ninth Amendment: Unenumerated rights retained by the people.
  10. Tenth Amendment: Powers reserved to the states or the people.

Modern Application (2026)

The Bill of Rights is the foundation of US constitutional law on:

  • Religious freedom (Sherbert v Verner 1963; Burwell v Hobby Lobby 2014)
  • Free speech (Brandenburg v Ohio 1969; Citizens United v FEC 2010)
  • Gun rights (District of Columbia v Heller 2008; NYSRPA v Bruen 2022)
  • Privacy (Griswold v Connecticut 1965; Roe v Wade 1973; Dobbs v Jackson 2022)
  • Criminal procedure (Miranda v Arizona 1966; Gideon v Wainwright 1963)
  • Punishment limits (Gregg v Georgia 1976; Kennedy v Louisiana 2008)
