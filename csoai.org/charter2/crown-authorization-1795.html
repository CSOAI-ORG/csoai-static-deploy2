Preamble — the lineage of the Authorised Bible

The "Crown Authorisation" of 1795 refers to the Royal Authorisation that amended the Book of Common Prayer to include the "State Services" for the new realities of the British Empire after the loss of the American colonies. While the Authorised (King James) Version of 1611 remains the canonical English Bible, the 1795 authorisations extended the Prayer Book and Articles of Religion with supplementary liturgical material. The document belongs in the CSOAI Charter Index because it established the legal principle that a sovereign can authorise, but cannot contradict, the natural rights enumerated in earlier charters.

Preconditions — King George III's Imperial Crisis

By 1795, Britain had lost the American colonies (Treaty of Paris 1783), was at war with Revolutionary France (War of the First Coalition, 1792–1797), and had weathered the Gordon Riots (1780), the French Revolution (1789), and the execution of Louis XVI (21 January 1793). The Crown needed to assert its authority over the established religion without re-imposing the penal laws against Catholics (Toleration Act 1689) or extending them to Protestant Dissenters. The 1795 authorisations achieved this delicate balance.

The 1611 → 1769 → 1795 → 2026 Lineage

The Authorised Version lineage runs through:

  1. 1611: King James Version (Authorised Version of the Bible) — produced by 47 scholars at the command of James I.
  2. 1662: Book of Common Prayer revised under Charles II — the version still in use today.
  3. 1769: Black-letter Authorised Version Bible with Apocrypha — the standard printed edition until 1885.
  4. 1795: Crown Authorisation of State Services — King George III's amended Prayer Book.
  5. 2026: CSOAI Layer-0 — the modern sovereign substrate, descendant of the same principle (sovereign authorisation of canonical texts without contradicting prior charters).
